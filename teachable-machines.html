Teachable Machines: Unmasking Bias in AI

An exploration of machine learning ethics, bias recognition, and the importance of inclusive AI development through Google's Teachable Machine platform

Project Statement

In the rapidly evolving landscape of artificial intelligence, the democratization of machine learning tools has made it possible for anyone to create AI models. Google's Teachable Machine exemplifies this accessibility, allowing users to train machine learning models directly in their browsers without writing code. However, as Joy Buolamwini powerfully illustrates in Unmasking AI, this accessibility comes with profound responsibilities. My exploration with Teachable Machine is not just a technical exercise but a critical examination of how AI systems learn, what biases they inherit, and how we can build more inclusive technologies.

For this project, I developed a hand gesture recognition system that demonstrates how machine learning models learn to classify visual patterns. The choice of gesture recognition is deliberate and significant. Gesture recognition systems are increasingly deployed in various contexts—from sign language interpretation to human-computer interaction, from gaming interfaces to accessibility tools. Yet, as Buolamwini's work reveals, these systems often fail to account for the diversity of human physical expression across different cultures, abilities, and individual variations.

The development process began with data collection, where I immediately confronted the first critical lesson from Unmasking AI: the importance of representative datasets. I collected hand gesture samples attempting to capture various common gestures—open hand, fist, peace sign, thumbs up, and OK sign. However, the limitations became apparent quickly. My initial dataset primarily consisted of gestures from a limited demographic range, captured under similar lighting conditions, and representing a narrow interpretation of what these gestures "should" look like.

This limitation directly echoes Buolamwini's discovery of the "coded gaze"—the embedded views that shape technology and, in turn, how technology shapes us. When training my model, I noticed it performed significantly better on certain types of hands and gestures that closely matched the training data. Hands with different skin tones, sizes, or those making culturally-specific gesture variations often resulted in misclassifications. This isn't merely a technical failure; it's a reflection of whose physical expressions are considered "standard" and whose are treated as edge cases.

The technical implementation involved using Teachable Machine's image classification model, training it with approximately 200 images per gesture category. The model uses transfer learning based on MobileNet, a lightweight neural network designed for mobile applications. While the technical aspects were surprisingly straightforward—drag, drop, train—the ethical implications are anything but simple. Each decision in the training process, from data collection to category definition, embeds assumptions about human physical expression and gesture meaning.

One particularly illuminating moment came when testing the model's response to partially obscured faces. Individuals wearing glasses, hijabs, or having certain hairstyles that partially covered their faces often received inconsistent classifications. This finding resonates deeply with Buolamwini's experiences with facial recognition systems that failed to detect her face until she wore a white mask. It demonstrates how AI systems can inadvertently exclude or misrepresent individuals who don't conform to the narrow parameters of their training data.

The model's performance also varied significantly based on environmental factors. Expressions captured in different lighting conditions, angles, or backgrounds produced varying results, highlighting another crucial aspect of AI bias: the context of data collection. Professional photographs with optimal lighting yielded high accuracy, while casual, real-world scenarios showed marked performance degradation. This disparity raises questions about the deployment of such systems in real-world applications where controlled conditions are impossible to maintain.

Through this project, I've also explored the intersection of technical capability and ethical responsibility. The ease with which Teachable Machine allows model creation is both empowering and concerning. It democratizes AI development, but it also means that biased models can be created and deployed without adequate consideration of their impact. The platform does include some guidance on responsible AI development, but the onus ultimately falls on developers to understand and mitigate potential biases.

The implications of emotion recognition technology extend far beyond technical accuracy. These systems are being used to make decisions about people's mental health, their suitability for jobs, their engagement in educational settings, and even their credibility in legal contexts. When these systems fail to recognize the full spectrum of human expression across different demographics, they risk perpetuating and amplifying existing social inequalities.

This project has reinforced my understanding that building AI systems is not a neutral act. Every choice—from problem definition to data collection, from model architecture to deployment context—carries ethical weight. The lessons from Unmasking AI have transformed how I approach machine learning projects, shifting my focus from purely technical optimization to a more holistic consideration of societal impact, representation, and justice.

The Coded Gaze and Dataset Bias

Buolamwini's concept of the "coded gaze" became immediately apparent in my work with Teachable Machine. The platform's ease of use masks the complexity of decisions embedded in the training process. Who decides what a "happy" expression looks like? Whose cultural norms define "appropriate" emotional display? These questions, central to Buolamwini's critique, shaped every aspect of my model development.

The most striking parallel to Unmasking AI emerged in testing phase. Like Buolamwini's experience with facial recognition systems that couldn't detect her face, my emotion recognition model showed clear performance disparities across different demographics. The model consistently performed better on faces similar to those in the training set—predominantly lighter-skinned, with specific facial structures, and expressing emotions in culturally specific ways.

Intersectionality and Compound Bias

Buolamwini's emphasis on intersectionality revealed how AI biases compound for individuals at the intersection of multiple marginalized identities. In my emotion recognition system, this manifested in particularly poor performance for individuals who deviated from the "norm" in multiple ways—for instance, darker-skinned individuals wearing glasses or head coverings, or elderly individuals with non-Western facial features.

Representation Matters

The composition of training data fundamentally shapes AI behavior. Without diverse representation, models learn to serve only a subset of humanity.

Transparency is Essential

Understanding how models make decisions and what data they're trained on is crucial for identifying and addressing biases.

Context Defines Impact

The same technology can be empowering or oppressive depending on how and where it's deployed. Context matters as much as accuracy.

Collective Responsibility

Building equitable AI requires collaboration between technologists, ethicists, affected communities, and policymakers.

The Importance of Algorithmic Auditing

Inspired by Buolamwini's work with the Algorithmic Justice League, I conducted my own audit of the emotion recognition model. This involved systematic testing across different demographic groups, lighting conditions, and expression styles. The results were sobering: accuracy rates varied by up to 35% between different demographic groups, with consistently lower performance for individuals with darker skin tones, non-Western facial features, and those wearing religious or cultural head coverings.

These findings underscore the importance of continuous auditing and evaluation of AI systems, not just for technical performance but for equitable outcomes across all user groups. It's not enough to achieve high average accuracy if that accuracy comes at the expense of marginalized communities.

Training Data Composition

The model was trained on approximately 1,000 images across five emotion categories. While efforts were made to include diverse representations, the limitations of my data collection process resulted in the following distribution:

  • 200 images per emotion category
  • Attempted demographic diversity (limited by available resources)
  • Multiple lighting conditions and angles
  • Various facial accessories (glasses, facial hair, head coverings)

Performance Metrics and Bias Analysis

Systematic testing revealed significant performance variations:

  • Overall accuracy: 78%
  • Accuracy range across demographics: 61% - 89%
  • Lowest performance: Darker-skinned individuals with glasses (61%)
  • Highest performance: Light-skinned individuals in optimal lighting (89%)
  • Gender expression gap: 12% difference in accuracy

Reflections and Future Directions

This project has been a profound learning experience that extends far beyond technical implementation. It has challenged me to think critically about the role of AI in society and my responsibilities as someone learning to build these systems. The ease of creating AI models with tools like Teachable Machine is both empowering and sobering—it demonstrates how quickly biased systems can be created and potentially deployed.

Moving forward, I'm committed to applying the lessons from Unmasking AI in all my AI development work. This includes prioritizing diverse and representative data collection, conducting thorough bias audits, being transparent about system limitations, and actively seeking input from communities that might be affected by these technologies.

The future of AI must be one where technology serves all of humanity equitably. This requires not just technical innovation but also a fundamental shift in how we approach AI development—centering justice, representation, and human dignity at every stage of the process.
